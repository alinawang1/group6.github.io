	<h2 class="section-heading text-uppercase">Annotated Bibliography</h2>
</div>
Cantor, Jonathan, Deborah A. Cohen, Julia Caldwell, and Tony Kuo. "Neighborhood Environments, SNAP-Ed Eligibility, and Health Behaviors: An Analysis of the California Health Interview Survey (CHIS)." *Journal of Urban Health* 97 (2020): 543–51. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11524-020-00433-x.

<p class="bib-access"><em>Accessed via DOI link above (Springer).</em></p>
<p class="bib-annotation">Cantor and colleagues argue that a neighborhood's overall income level shapes residents' health behaviors independently of their own individual income. They support this claim using CHIS survey data on walking behavior, sugary beverage consumption, and obesity, cross-referenced against SNAP-Ed neighborhood eligibility maps across California. The study is important because it complicates simple income-based explanations of health disparities, showing that where someone lives can matter as much as what they earn. This reinforces the argument that the reasons CHIS respondents gave for delaying medication, stockouts, insurance friction, and communication breakdowns reflect structural and environmental conditions rather than individual neglect, paralleling Cantor's finding that context drives outcomes independent of the person.</p>

Dallo, Federico, Thomas Parkinson, Carlos Duarte, Stefano Schiavon, Chai Yoon Um, Mark P. Modera, Paul Raftery, Carlo Barbante, and Brett C. Singer. "Using Smart Thermostats to Reduce Indoor Exposure to Wildfire Fine Particulate Matter (PM2.5)." *Indoor Environments* 2, no. 2 (2025): 100088. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.indenv.2025.100088.

<p class="bib-access"><em>Accessed via the DOI link above.</em></p>
<p class="bib-annotation">This article argues that smart thermostats and central air systems could be used more effectively to lower people's exposure to dangerous wildfire smoke inside their homes. The researchers use smart thermostat data from around 5,000 California homes during the 2020 wildfire season, as well as computer models that estimate how different air-conditioning and filtration settings would affect indoor PM2.5 levels. This source is important because it shows that existing household technology could become a practical public health tool, especially during increasingly common wildfire events. It supports our project by showing how environmental health risks are also connected to housing conditions, income, and access to protective technology, which could also support our potential policy recommendations. However, because many of the estimated improvements come from modeling rather than direct indoor measurements in every home, the results mainly show what these systems could potentially achieve.</p>

Do, V., C. Chen, T. Benmarhnia, and J. A. Casey. 2024. "Spatial Heterogeneity of the Respiratory Health Impacts of Wildfire Smoke PM2.5 in California." *GeoHealth* 8, no. 4 (2024): e2023GH000997. https://doi.org/10.1029/2023GH000997.

<p class="bib-access"><em>Accessed via DOI link above (Wiley).</em></p>
<p class="bib-annotation">Do et al. (2024) argue that statewide approaches are inefficient and should be upgraded to community-oriented strategies for the most optimal care. In Do et al.'s research, they used wildfire-specific PM2.5 air pollution estimates and respiratory emergency department visits, along with hospitalization records from 2006 to 2019, to examine how health varies from area to area. They found that the effects of wildfire smoke differed greatly throughout the state, with the greatest impacts being in communities that have higher proportions of Black and Pacific Islander residents, while the communities with lower impacts were those that had access to technology like air conditioning and more open space. This shows that people with lower incomes are facing harsher living conditions and more severe health risks because they often lack the resources needed to better protect themselves from wildfire smoke. This connects back to our claim that healthcare disparities are not evenly distributed across California. One limitation of the study is that it mainly focuses on respiratory health and does not discuss other health effects caused by wildfire smoke.</p>

Dubowitz, Tamara, Shannon N. Zenk, Bonnie Ghosh-Dastidar, et al. 2015. "Healthy Food Access for Urban Food Desert Residents: Examination of the Food Environment, Food Purchasing Practices, Diet and BMI." *Public Health Nutrition* (Cambridge, UK) 18, no. 12 (2015): 2220–30. https://doi.org/10.1017/S1368980014002742.

<p class="bib-access"><em>Accessed through the DOI link included in the citation.</em></p>
<p class="bib-annotation">Dubowitz et al. argue that distance from the nearest supermarkets does not necessarily explain why people have poor diets or higher BMI's among residents of food deserts, suggesting that there may be other factors that contribute to poor diet quality among residents. This study extracted data based on household surveys, dietary recalls, geographic distance data, and more to make their argument. This article strengthens our thesis by revealing how access is shaped by many other complexities such as affordability, types of food available, transportation, etc. This is useful in our argument in our prevention section, as though health resources may exist, there are social and economic conditions that determine who can benefit from them. California cannot reduce health inequality just by placing resources in communities but has to consider if these resources are affordable to meet people's needs.</p>

Faiz, Jessica, Mariah Blegen, Vanessa Nuñez, Daniel Gonzalez, Daniel C. Stokes, Kevin Truong, Gery Ryan, Medell Briggs-Malonson, and Katherine L. Kahn. "Frontline Perspectives on Barriers to Care for Patients with California Medicaid: A Qualitative Study." *International Journal for Equity in Health* 23 (2024): 102. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12939-024-02174-8.

<p class="bib-access"><em>Accessed via the DOI link above.</em></p>
<p class="bib-annotation">This article argues that having Medi-Cal insurance does not always mean that patients can actually receive timely and coordinated healthcare. The researchers interviewed nineteen clinicians and administrative staff members at a large California academic medical center about problems involving scheduling, referrals, insurance authorization, contracting, and clinical visits. This source is important because it explains the specific processes behind delayed appointments, as well as the difficulty reaching specialists instead of simply reporting that Medicaid patients have worse access. It directly supports our project's argument that insurance coverage alone is not enough to measure healthcare access, since the type of plan and the way the healthcare system handles it also matter. A limitation is that the interviews came from employees at only one academic medical center and did not directly include patients' perspectives, so the experiences may not represent every Medi-Cal patient or healthcare system.</p>

Guendelman, Sylvia, Veronica Angulo, and Doug Oman. "Access to Health Care for Children and Adolescents in Working Poor Families: Recent Findings From California." *Medical Care* 43, no. 1 (2005): 68–78. https://www.jstor.org/stable/3768345?sid=primo.

<p class="bib-access"><em>Accessed through the JSTOR link included in the citation.</em></p>
<p class="bib-annotation">Guendelman et al. argue that even though public insurance expansions improved coverage, children from low-income families still face disparities in continuous access to insurance, usual source of care, and other health services. In addition, socioeconomic barriers such as provider availability and social conditions limit healthcare access even for insured families. The study is relevant to our research because it supports our third theme of healthcare access and institutional barriers. This section of our project examines whether insurance leads to a usual source of care and timely appointments. The article shows that insurance alone does not lead to equal access to healthcare. However, one limitation is that the article focuses on children, while we decided to focus on health disparities among adults. Although some of the barriers may be similar for both children and adults, it is difficult to apply the findings from the article directly to our project.</p>

Hacker, Karen, Craig W. Thomas, Guixiang Zhao, J'Neka S. Claxton, Paul Eke, and Machell Town. "Social Determinants of Health and Health-Related Social Needs among Adults with Chronic Diseases in the United States, Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System, 2022." *Preventing Chronic Disease* 21 (2024): E94. https://doi.org/10.5888/pcd21.240362.

<p class="bib-access"><em>Accessed via the DOI link above.</em></p>
<p class="bib-annotation">This article argues that adults with more chronic health conditions are also more likely to experience social and economic difficulties that can make their health harder to manage. The authors use responses from more than 324,000 adults in the 2022 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System to examine nine chronic diseases and twelve measures of social needs, including medical costs, food insecurity, stress, housing, and transportation. This source is important because it uses a very large dataset to show that health cannot be understood only through diagnoses or individual behavior. It supports our project by giving evidence that cost barriers, mental stress, and other social conditions may help explain differences in healthcare access and health outcomes. One limitation is that the survey relies on self-reported information and does not include every state, so it cannot prove that the social needs directly caused the chronic conditions.</p>

Jones, Caitlin G., Ana G. Rappold, Jason Vargo, Wayne E. Cascio, Martin Kharrazi, Bryan McNally, Sumi Hoshiko, and the CARES Surveillance Group. "Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrests and Wildfire-Related Particulate Matter During 2015–2017 California Wildfires." *Journal of the American Heart Association* 9, no. 8 (2020): e014125. https://doi.org/10.1161/JAHA.119.014125.

<p class="bib-access"><em>Accessed through the DOI link included in the citation.</em></p>
<p class="bib-annotation">Jones et al. argue that breathing in smoke from heavy wildfire significantly increases the risk of cardiac arrest, with socioeconomic status being a factor. To examine this relationship, the article examines wildfire smoke exposure and cardiac arrests that occurred outside of the hospital across fourteen California counties between 2015 and 2017. In particular, individuals living in marginalized communities appear to be more vulnerable because they have fewer resources to protect themselves from smoke exposure through resources such as air filtration and even relocation. According to the article, wildfire smoke does not seem like an equal environmental hazard for everyone because social conditions make some people more vulnerable than others. The article is relevant to our research because it supports one of our levels of health inequality, which is people's ability to protect themselves from environmental hazards. Our group uses CHIS data to compare wildfire smoke exposure and access to filtered air across different socioeconomic groups, and this study shows evidence that unequal protective measures can lead to unequal outcomes. More broadly, the article shows that health disparities do not simply result from differences in exposure but also from differences in the available resources, which is the underlying theme of our website.</p>

Koman, Patricia D., Michael Billmire, Kirk R. Baker, Ricardo de Majo, Frank J. Anderson, Sumi Hoshiko, Brian J. Thelen, and Nancy H. F. French. "Mapping Modeled Exposure of Wildland Fire Smoke for Human Health Studies in California." *Atmosphere* 10, no. 6 (2019): 308. https://doi.org/10.3390/atmos10060308.

<p class="bib-access"><em>Accessed via DOI link above (MDPI open access).</em></p>
<p class="bib-annotation">Koman et al. argue that wildfire smoke exposure across California is both widespread and geographically uneven, with some counties experiencing far more high-smoke "smokewave" days than others. They support this using satellite-derived PM2.5 modeling and county-level exposure estimates from 2007-2013. The study is important because it establishes, with hard data, that wildfire smoke exposure is a measurable and unevenly distributed public health burden rather than a uniform statewide risk. This provides the environmental baseline behind our group's "Protection" theme, and parallels our finding that medication-delay barriers are also unevenly distributed. In our case, by age group rather than geography, but following the same broader pattern of unequal exposure to structural health risks.</p>

Lai, Tianjian, Brian Tuohy, and Roger Waldinger. "Legal Status Disparities in Preventive Care Usage among Latino Immigrants in California: A Cross-Sectional Analysis." *PLOS Global Public Health* 5, no. 7 (2025): e0004855. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgph.0004855.

<p class="bib-access"><em>Accessed via DOI link above (PLOS open access).</em></p>
<p class="bib-annotation">Lai, Tuohy, and Waldinger argue that legal status itself, independent of insurance coverage, creates a barrier to preventive healthcare use among Latino immigrants in California. They support this using a cross-sectional analysis of CHIS 2015-2016 data covering roughly 5,500 foreign-born Latino respondents, comparing preventive care usage across legal status categories. The study is important because it shows that "access to care" is not solved by insurance coverage alone, which complicates policy responses that focus only on expanding coverage. This directly supports our finding that medication delay in the CHIS data is driven by more than insurance status: only two of thirteen reasons respondents gave ("insurance approval issue" and "no insurance") were coverage-related, while the rest — stockouts, provider communication, cost, and forgetting — point to the same multidimensional access problem Lai's team identifies.</p>

Meyer, Oanh L., Laura Castro-Schilo, and Sergio Aguilar-Gaxiola. "Determinants of Mental Health and Self-Rated Health: A Model of Socioeconomic Status, Neighborhood Safety, and Physical Activity." *American Journal of Public Health* 104, no. 9 (2014): 1734–41. https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.2014.302003.

<p class="bib-access"><em>Accessed through the DOI link included in the citation.</em></p>
<p class="bib-annotation">Meyer et al. argue that socioeconomic status, neighborhood safety, and physical activity all influence mental health and self-rated health among California adults. The main argument is that lower socioeconomic status leads to greater concerns about neighborhood safety, which discourages physical activities and leads to poorer health outcomes. The study shows that living conditions, separate from healthcare, significantly influence health disparities. This article is relevant to our research because it supports our second theme of housing and everyday health. In our project, we explore how housing instability is associated with neighborhood safety, physical activity, and other daily challenges. The article provides evidence that socioeconomic conditions and health are closely connected and even supports the wildfire article by showing that inequality is also caused by living conditions. One concern we have is that the article uses data from 2009 only, which makes it difficult to see if the relationship between differences in socioeconomic status and differences in health outcomes is causal.</p>

Palinkas, Lawrence A., Jessenia De Leon, Kexin Yu, et al. 2023. "Adaptation Resources and Responses to Wildfire Smoke and Other Forms of Air Pollution in Low-Income Urban Settings: A Mixed-Methods Study." *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health* 20, no. 7 (2023): 5393. https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph20075393.

<p class="bib-access"><em>Accessed via DOI link above (MDPI open access).</em></p>
<p class="bib-annotation">Palinkas et al. (2023) argue that people who are actively trying to improve their health are still incomparable to those who have broader public health support. The study examines 40 adults in Los Angeles who reside in neighborhoods with both high air pollution and socioeconomic vulnerability. The participants discuss the problems of living in these conditions and reveal problems such as respiratory symptoms, headaches, along with the common stress and anxiety combination during periods of poor air quality. They also reveal that they take precautions to protect themselves by wearing masks and staying indoors to reduce exposure, but explain that their protection still comes up short because they lack the financial capability and resources to fully protect themselves. This connects back to our thesis that socioeconomic status is a large factor in the amount of resources and healthcare one gets. One limitation of the study is that it only examined 40 participants from Los Angeles, so its findings may not represent all low-income communities across California.</p>

Pourat, N., E. Chuang, Y.-Y. Meng, A. E. Martinez, and G. F. Kominski. 2022. "How Has Access to Care for Medi-Cal Enrollees Fared Relative to Employer-Sponsored Insurance 4 Years After the Affordable Care Act Expansion?" *Journal of General Internal Medicine* 37, no. 9 (2022): 2150–57. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11606-021-07383-3.

<p class="bib-access"><em>Accessed via DOI link above (Springer).</em></p>
<p class="bib-annotation">Pourat et al. (2022) argue that even though the Affordable Care Act granted more people access to healthcare, those who had healthcare sponsored by their employers still prevailed, as Medi-Cal enrollees still had smaller appointment rates. The research was conducted using data from the California Health Interview Survey and compared healthcare access between two groups 4 years before the Affordable Care Act expansion. The results revealed that access to further healthcare was significantly lower due to long wait times and limited availability of specialists and advanced facilities, compared to those with employer insurance. The study gives us insight that it wasn't that the people were not attempting to get healthcare, and that simply having insurance doesn't guarantee the patient equal access to healthcare, even if they were going out of their way to get it. This further backs up our project's claim, as health outcomes are affected by more than just insurance coverage.</p>

"Public Well-Being Value of a UC Degree." University of California Office of the President, 2011. https://www.ucop.edu/institutional-research-academic-planning/_files/vod-public-well-being-brief.pdf.

<p class="bib-access"><em>Accessed via link above (UC Office of the President PDF).</em></p>
<p class="bib-annotation">This brief tracks how UC alumni contribute to public well-being through their careers, and a good chunk of that ends up mapping onto the healthcare access piece. It reports that over 30,000 UC-trained doctors and nurses are practicing in California, and that UC-trained mental health professionals and social workers are serving 32 of the state's 58 counties. It is important to note that workforce supply is really the other half of the access story we've been building; even if someone has coverage, they still need an actual provider nearby, and this source shows UC is a major pipeline for that supply. Furthermore, to my surprise, this brief ties heavily into the environmental-hazards level of our framework. It highlights UC alumni behind tools like MyShake, the state's earthquake early-warning app, and AUDREY, a wildfire-response system built for NASA that gives firefighters real-time hazard data. Both are direct examples of research aimed at helping people protect themselves before a disaster hits, which is the kind of upstream protection our research is about. This source is useful for our project because it shows how environmental hazards and healthcare access aren't separate systems; they're often staffed and built by the same institutional pipeline, in this case, UC.</p>

Saeed, Sy Atezaz, and Ross MacRae Masters. 2021. "Disparities in Health Care and the Digital Divide." *Current Psychiatry Reports* (New York) 23, no. 9 (2021): 61. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11920-021-01274-4.

<p class="bib-access"><em>Accessed through the DOI link included in the citation.</em></p>
<p class="bib-annotation">Saeed and Masters argue that although telehealth can improve health care, it can worsen the existing inequalities when people do not have reliable access to technology and support. This research shows that low-income, Black patients, women, rural residents, and other marginalized communities are less likely to fully benefit from telehealth. The article uses resources from other review articles such as FCC data showing how 24 million Americans lack adequate broadband, previous telehealth studies, healthcare disparity research, and examples of racial and socioeconomic bias in digital health technologies. This is important — it shows that expanding telehealth does not automatically create equal healthcare access because many people are unable to access tech such as devices, digital literacy, internet, etc. This is relevant to our thesis because it demonstrates that making health services available is different from making them accessible in our health care access section. Health policies must address people's ability to use services, not only make those services unavailable.</p>

Soule, Eric K., Sinan Sousan, Jack Pender, et al. 2023. "Secondhand Electronic Cigarette Aerosol in Vehicles Impacts Indoor Air Quality." *Drug and Alcohol Dependence* (Ireland) 250 (2023): 110889. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2023.110889.

<p class="bib-access"><em>Accessed through the DOI link included in the citation.</em></p>
<p class="bib-annotation">Soule et al. argue that existing policies that restrict smoking in vehicles should also restrict e-cigarette use. Their research shows that vaping inside a vehicle can produce high concentrations of particulate matter, negatively impacting the air quality. It can further expose people, especially children, to secondhand e-cigarette aerosols. The researchers supported their argument by gathering 60 participants who used e-cigarettes to vape in their own vehicles to measure PM2.5 concentration with air monitors, recording the patterns of puffing. This resource is important because it supports the idea that secondhand e-smoking can threaten passengers' health. It strengthens our thesis by showing that many current health protections can still be incomplete, such as in this case, leaving passengers vulnerable to harm in our environmental protection section. Further revealing that policies may need other sources to reveal how different exposures can affect different social and economic groups.</p>

Stringer, Heather. "Medi-Cal Bold Idea — AI-Powered Advocates for Medi-Cal." *California Health Care Foundation*, 2026. https://www.chcf.org/resource/medi-cal-bold-idea-ai-first-medi-cal-health-data-utility/.

<p class="bib-access"><em>Accessed via link above (CHCF website).</em></p>
<p class="bib-annotation">In this article, writer Heather Stringer highlights how Medi-Cal only shows up for people after something's already gone wrong, like an ER visit or losing their housing, instead of catching problems early. Medi-Cal's fix is building AI "Advocates" into the state's existing data system so it can flag people before things get that bad, like noticing someone with diabetes hasn't refilled their insulin and reaching out before it turns into an ER trip. In the article, Stringer explains the story of a woman with diabetes who's also dealing with unstable housing ends up in the ER five times in six months because nobody follows up with her in between. She highlights how that's not really just an access problem — it's the individual's housing situation making it impossible to manage her health in the first place, which connects right back to the first two levels of our framework. Continuing, this source doesn't ignore the risks either. They bring up real concerns about bias in AI outreach, which makes the source more credible instead of just selling AI as some perfect fix. For our project, this article provides a concrete, real-world example of what an access-level intervention could actually look like. It helps make the case that fixing access alone probably isn't enough if the deeper stuff, like housing and everyday living conditions, never gets addressed.</p>

Tolbert, Jennifer, Sammy Cervantes, Clea Bell, and Anthony Danica. "Key Facts about the Uninsured Population." *KFF*, June 16, 2026. https://www.kff.org/uninsured/key-facts-about-the-uninsured-population/.

<p class="bib-access"><em>Accessed via link above (KFF website).</em></p>
<p class="bib-annotation">This source is focused on who lacks insurance coverage and what that actually costs them. According to KFF's analysis of 2024 American Community Survey data, 26.7 million people under 65 were uninsured that year, and for the first time since 2019, that number went up instead of down. What matters most for our research question is who this affects: over eight in ten uninsured people are in low-income families, and nearly two-thirds are people of color, with American Indian, Alaska Native, and Hispanic individuals facing uninsured rates more than double the rate for White people. That's not a coincidence, and it lines up with the idea that access barriers don't exist on their own; they build on top of the income and employment disparities we're covering at the everyday-living-conditions level, because most uninsured adults are actually working but simply aren't offered coverage through their job. The consequences are stark, too: uninsured adults with chronic conditions were three to four times more likely to delay care due to cost than insured adults with the same condition, and nearly half hadn't seen a doctor in the past year at all. For our project, this source gives us the clearest evidence that lacking access to healthcare isn't just an inconvenience, but a mechanism that actively deepens the disparities created at the earlier levels of our framework. This later turns unequal starting conditions into unequal health outcomes.</p>
